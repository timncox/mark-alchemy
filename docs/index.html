Heard, and still being tested

On 22 September 2026 it ran on a racked module: loops record and play back, the TRACK page works, B2 shows clock lock, and the SD picker switches between Mark, Smack and Belt with each keeping its own settings. Five tracks of 55 seconds each fit the module's SDRAM, and the native tests pass.

What it does

01 — The idea

The Boss RC-505's workflow on a Eurorack module: five independent stereo loop tracks, each stepping record → play → overdub under one button, stop under the other, synced to a shared bar, with undo. What you patch into it keeps passing through underneath, and each of the five light rings is that track's display.

The rings

Five track displays

An RC-505 has five columns of coloured lights. This module has five rings around five pots. Each ring is its track: red recording, green playing, yellow overdubbing, white stopped, with a playhead running round it.

The bar

Your first loop sets it

With nothing on the clock jack, the first loop you record is exactly as long as you played it and becomes the bar every later loop snaps to. TEMPO only tells the module how many bars that was. With a clock on J3, everything snaps to that instead.

Two buttons

A track button and a stop button

Pick a track with the TRACK SELECT pot. B1 is that track's REC/PLAY: tap through record, play and overdub, hold for undo, hold longer to clear. B2 stops it; hold B2 for ALL.

It is the same C engine as Mark for the Ableton Move, recompiled for the Alchemy Lab: six pots on three pages, three buttons, six CV jacks that read independently of the pots, and 102 LEDs. Each track's one-shot, reverse, built-in effect and pan are on the TRACK page, a tap of B3 away. The one thing the Move version has that this panel does not is sessions saved to the card; the engine still has them. It also brings firmware swapping from the SD card, so this module can carry several firmwares and change between them from a settings page.

Playing it

03 — Five steps to the first two loops
1  Patch audio to J1 / J2 (IN). It passes through immediately.
2  Turn TRACK SELECT (P6, bottom right) to 1. The B1 light shows track 1's state: dim, empty.
3  Tap B1. The P1 ring turns red. It is recording, right now,
   because nothing is playing yet and nothing is clocking it.
4  Tap B1 again where the bar should end. The ring turns green
   and the loop plays. That length is now the bar.
5  Turn TRACK SELECT to 2, tap B1. The P2 ring blinks red until
   the bar line, then records for a bar and turns green on the next one.

Tap B1 on a playing track and it overdubs: the ring goes yellow and everything you play is layered onto the loop until you tap again. Tap B2 to stop a track; tap B1 to restart it on the next bar line. Hold B2 for ALL: every playing track stops, or every stopped track starts, on the spot.

Hold B1 past half a second for undo. The last thing you did to the loop, a first recording or an overdub, comes back off; hold again and it comes back on. The B1 light turns white the moment the hold has reached undo, so you know before you let go. Hold two seconds to clear the track.

Taps act when you let go, so a hold never fires the tap first. The cost is a release-time delay on the first loop's downbeat, and the grid then carries that consistently; every later loop lines up with it.

What "the bar" means here

The engine follows one grid. A running clock on J3 wins. Otherwise the first finalized loop anchors the bar. Otherwise, the tempo. So unclocked, your first loop is not quantized at all: it is as long as your two taps, and the module divides it by the TEMPO pot's bar to decide how many bars it was, one at 120 BPM for a two-second loop, two for four seconds. Everything after that snaps to that bar, or to the fraction of it that QUANTIZE picks.

That is the RC-505 with no rhythm running, and it is why the clock jack is handled the way it is.

The other pages

B3 does two things. Hold it and the same six pots are the RC-505's system menu: the grid, what a recording ends in, how an overdub lands, whether tracks are sections, the tempo and the master. Tap it and they become the selected track's own page: one-shot, reverse, an effect, its amount and pan. Settings, the SDK's layer, sits behind both.

One page, five tracks. The page always shows the track TRACK points at. Turn P6, or let J6 move it, and the page reloads with that track's values; each pot then has to catch the new track's value before it moves it, so walking through the tracks never drags one track's settings onto the next. With the LEVEL rings out of sight, the P6 ring stands in for them: five dots in the five tracks' state colours, blinking while one waits for the bar line, the selected one at full brightness and the rest at a quarter. A tap is a press under 0.3 s that turned nothing and never met B2. The SETUP layer engages on the press, so SETUP flashes on the rings for the length of the tap. The B3 light says where the next tap lands you: dim violet on PLAY, dim amber on TRACK. The swatches here are brightened; on the module both are low-brightness tints.

First boot: every track Loop, Forward, FX off with the amount at 50, pan centre. Not here yet: loop length. The engine only accepts a length of up to one bar, so a pot for it would cut a longer loop down to a bar. Halving a loop needs a small engine change first.

Where it saves

Slot 13, Mark's own

Each firmware in the family autosaves to a slot of its own: Smack 12, Mark 13, Belt 14, Relay 15, counting from 0 (HostLink counts from 1). So switching to Belt and back leaves Mark as you left it. Slots 0–11 are for presets you save by hand. Saving one by hand into 12–15 overwrites that firmware's working state.

The clock

06 — J3, and why it is quiet until a pulse arrives

Patch a clock to J3 and every loop snaps to it: a recording ends on its bar line, a start waits for its bar line, and CLOCK RATIO says whether one pulse is a half note, a quarter or an eighth. Leave J3 empty and the module makes its own bar from your first loop.

J3 empty

The first loop is the bar

No quantizing until something is playing. Your first recording is as long as your taps, and TEMPO says how many bars it spanned. Later loops snap to it.

J3 clocked

The clock is the bar

Pulses become the 24-pulse-per-quarter clock the engine already speaks. Every record length rounds to the grid, every start waits for the bar line.

Settings P5

Ratio

÷2, =1 or ×2 on what arrives, so a 16th-note clock and a quarter-note clock both make a four-beat bar.

The engine hears the clock jack only after a pulse has arrived on it. The clock adapter this firmware shares with Smack free-runs at the TEMPO when nothing is patched, which is what Smack wants; Mark does not, because a free-running clock would quantize the first loop to the TEMPO pot and take the bar away from your hands. So nothing is forwarded until J3 fires, and when the pulse train stops the module sends the engine a single stop and goes quiet again; the engine then carries on with the bar it already has.

J3 is read raw in the audio callback, not through the SDK's 20 Hz pot filter, which would smear a pulse. It fires on the magnitude of the swing away from the jack's calibrated zero, 1.5 V to assert and 0.5 V to release, so a pulse counts whichever way the front end signs it. The same reader watches J4 and J5 for the two trigger inputs. It reads once per 128-sample block, about every 2.7 ms, so use pulses of 5 ms or longer: shorter ones can be missed.

Loops do not follow the clock's tempo. Playback is always at the speed it was recorded. The clock places record, play and stop on the bar line, rounds loop lengths to whole bars, and times the delay effect. Change the clock's tempo and the loops keep their own. B2 turning blue on a clock has been seen on the module. A long steady-clock run to check for drift has not been done yet.

Jacks and CV

07 — Two triggers, three CV inputs, independent of the pots

The Alchemy Lab reads pots and jacks separately and the SDK adds them in software, per page and per pot. Two of the six jacks are triggers here, because a looper whose record button a sequencer can press is a different instrument from one it cannot.

A sequencer into J6 and a trigger into J4 records, plays and overdubs across all five tracks with no hands on the module. The triggers are edge-detected in the audio callback and acted on at the one-millisecond control poll, so the engine's parameter calls never run on the audio thread.

The routing is fixed and provisional; choosing it from the Settings pages is the obvious next version.

The boot report

The load, shown when you can look

You cannot watch a status light with five loops running and both hands on the pots, so the module measures its own worst-case load while you play and shows it on the P1 ring for the first two and a half seconds of the next power-up.

A single dim blue LED means it has not measured anything yet. Green is under 75%, amber under 90%, red means it did not fit. Firmware on the card

09 — Carry several firmwares, change between them from the panel

Hermetic's firmware treats the SD card as a place for presets and files. This one also reads .bin files from it, and can write one into the module's firmware slot, verify it, and reboot into it, from a settings page, with the module on and in the rack. Smack and Belt for the Alchemy Lab carry the same page, so any of the three can hand the module to any other.

Prepare the card

FAT32, a folder named alchemy

32 GB or under, MBR partition table, FAT32. Make a folder called alchemy in the root and put firmware images in it: any .bin built for the Alchemy Lab bootloader, this one included. On a Mac, run dot_clean on the card before ejecting so the picker does not list resource forks as files.

Or over USB

Without pulling the card

Files reach the card over USB while the module runs: the web programmer's file panel, or the repository's tools/hostlink-fs.mjs, which makes the folder and puts the images in it from a terminal. Uploads are staged and renamed onto their final name only after the firmware has checked the CRC.

What it refuses, and what it never touches

It checks the image's first eight bytes before erasing anything and refuses one that is not bootable from SRAM. The largest file it will take is the app slot up to the calibration sector, so the board's calibration record and the preset store are never in the erase path. If a write fails, the module blinks red and boots nothing; the bootloader is intact, and hold-B3-at-power-on still gets you a USB flash.

What has run. On 22 September 2026 the picker switched a racked module between Mark, Smack and Belt. It listed the folder, wrote and verified the slot, rebooted, and skipped the write when the chosen file was already loaded. Each firmware came back with its own settings: Mark's are kept in slot 13 and are not touched while another firmware runs. The failure paths (a bad image, a pulled card) have not been exercised.

USB and controllers

10 — One front port, three jobs: the Mac, controllers, or a sound card

The front USB-C does one thing per power-up. By default it talks to the Mac: the web programmer, presets, the card. Switched over, the Lab becomes the USB host for a Launchpad Mini MK3, a Launch Control XL and a Haute42 gamepad, or a two-in, two-out USB audio interface.

The USB port setting

Settings page 1 · P6, bottom right

Hold B2 + B3 for two seconds to open Settings, and turn P6, the bottom-right pot of Settings page 1. It is a three-way selector:

ModeThe front USB-C is
MacHostLink, the default: the web programmer, presets and the card's files, with the module racked.
LaunchpadThe USB host for controllers. The Lab drives a Launchpad Mini MK3, a Launch Control XL and a Haute42, through a powered dongle; see below. No HostLink this boot.
AudioA class-compliant USB audio interface (USB Audio Class 1): 2 channels in from J1/J2, 2 out to J9/J10, 48 kHz, 24-bit, no driver needed on a Mac. The looper is bypassed: what the computer plays comes out of J9/J10, what comes in on J1/J2 goes to the computer. There is no volume control in the path and J9/J10 are DC-coupled, so a DAW can send CV through them. No HostLink and no controllers this boot.

One setting for the whole card, from the next power-up

The mode is kept on the SD card in /alchemy/usb.cfg, shared by every firmware on it: each one adopts the card's value at boot and writes a change back, so switching firmware in the picker keeps the mode. A change takes effect the next time the module powers up.

Hold B1 while powering up for Mac mode for that one boot, whatever the setting says: the way back to the web programmer without opening Settings. The port has one USB role at a time, so Audio mode means no controllers, and neither Launchpad nor Audio mode has HostLink. The bootloader's DFU (hold B3 at power-on) is unaffected.

Hooking up controllers

A powered USB-C dongle with a hub inside · up to three controllers at once
Power

The front port has no 5 V

The Lab's USB-C gives no bus power, so a controller needs it from somewhere else: a powered USB-C dongle with a USB hub inside. Power the dongle, plug it into the Lab's front USB-C, and plug the controllers into the dongle. The one it was developed with carries a hub and an Ethernet chip; a device that is not a controller, like the Ethernet chip, is switched off and skipped.

Behind the hub

Three at once, one hub deep

Up to three controllers at a time, full speed. Free ports are rescanned every half second, so a controller can go in after power-up. Unplugging one mid-session is not handled tidily: the host sees errors and may reset, so power down to rearrange. The gamepad is only recognised behind a hub, never plugged straight in.

Button positions

The positions here follow the standard GP2040-CE hitbox mapping. If the controller's button mapping has been changed, the same effects follow the XInput button names (X, Y, RB, LB, A, B, RT, LT, the directions, L3, R3), not the physical positions. Start, Back and Guide do nothing.

Installing

12 — Through the front USB-C, and getting back to stock

The Alchemy Lab's bootloader serves DFU on the front-panel USB-C, so the module stays in the rack. Once this firmware is on it, so does every update after.

First time

Hold B3 at power-on

During the two-second boot window the rings run a warm-white comet. Hold B3 through it and they switch to a slow breathe with the B3 lights on: update mode. Then flash the .bin with Hermetic's web programmer, or from the repository:

make program-dfu
Every time after

Live

With Mark running, the module can be asked to reboot into update mode over HostLink and flashed in the same breath, no button held:

make program-live

Or put the new .bin on the card and use the picker.

Back to stock

Same door

Flashing any Alchemy Lab firmware, Hermetic's own included, the same way returns the module to it. Presets in the slots survive; this firmware's working state lives in slot 13 and is schema-guarded, so another firmware will not read it as its own.

Before you flash it

It is custom firmware. Hermetic Modular did not write it, did not test it and cannot support it, so please do not contact them about it. It has been played on one racked module, and the tests listed at the top are still open. If you run it, the things worth reporting are whether loops drift against a steady clock, whether J4 and J5 fire reliably, and what the boot report reads after a hard session with the TRACK-page effects on.

How it is built

13 — The parts worth knowing about

The engine's C file was not touched. Everything below is the layer between it and the Alchemy SDK, and the handful of decisions that shaped that layer.

PieceWhat it is
The engine, at 48 kHzMark's engine, byte-identical to the Move build's, with two edits in its header: the sample rate, because libDaisy offers no 44.1 kHz, and a build-time track capacity. 128-frame blocks, as the engine expects.
Its Move-only needs, stubbedThe engine uses threads for sessions and dynamic loading for external effects, neither of which exists on this board. Four small headers shadow the POSIX ones it includes and a stub file makes every such call fail the way the real one fails without the resource, so those features go inert instead of being cut out of the engine.
Five tracks in SDRAMA bump allocator in external RAM, 62 MiB of 64. The engine's own capacity ladder cannot work on it, because the allocator never gives memory back, so the capacity is fixed at build time and a native test proves it fits on the first attempt: 64.7 MB used, 55 seconds a track.
Three pages, one layerThe SDK's Pager makes SETUP a held layer on B3 with pot catch on the way in and out. Nothing on the PLAY page can jump because you looked at another one. A short B3 press is taken as a tap by the app, not the Pager (the Pager allows one binding per button), and flips PLAY and TRACK with GoToPage.
One TRACK page, five tracksThe Pager keeps one value per page and pot, so the five tracks' values live in the firmware's extras and the page is a window on the selected one. A track change reloads the page and re-arms catch; a preset load pushes all twenty-five values to the engine again. A native test checks the round trip and that one corrupt byte resets only that track.
Clock adapterGate edges become 24 ppqn MIDI clock, the same adapter as Smack, gated so the engine hears it only after a pulse and gets a stop when the pulses end.
Working presetSlot 13 (0-based) is Mark's own live state: all three pages, every track's TRACK-page values, the Settings and the CPU peak. It autosaves five seconds after the last change, PLAY levels included, once hands are off the buttons and the picker is idle, one 4 KB sector per save. At boot the home slot wins; slot 0, where earlier builds saved, is read once so that an older module keeps its settings. Loop audio is never saved. Adding the TRACK page changed the saved format, so the first boot of this build after a v0.1.0 one starts from first-boot defaults. That is the schema check doing its job.
The pickerThree Custom slots on a Settings page: a file scanner, a state machine that compares, erases, writes and verifies one step per frame, and a DFU hand-off. Shared, by copy, with Smack and Belt.
HostLinkProduct "Mark", all three pages, ten jacks, three buttons, a manual and the SD filesystem, over the front USB-C.
Budget~346 KB image. SRAM 72%, DTCM 76,300 B, SDRAM 97%. CPU: not yet read with the TRACK-page effects running.

Found something, or want it to do something it doesn't? Open an issue. A report from someone playing it is worth more than anything measurable on a laptop.
